## 1. Background

### 1. Recurrences and general terms of sequences

In mathematics, a sequence is an ordered collection of numbers, usually written $a_1, a_2, a_3, \ldots$. A sequence can be defined by a recurrence, for example $a_{n+1} = pa_n + q^n$. A recurrence gives the relationship between neighbouring terms. A general term, by contrast, is a direct expression for any term of the sequence, usually written $a_n$.

### 2. A first look at matrices

A matrix is a data structure in the form of a table, often used to represent linear transformations. The basic matrix operations are addition, scalar multiplication and matrix multiplication. Adding two matrices of the same size adds the entries in matching positions; scalar multiplication multiplies every entry by the same scalar; matrix multiplication multiplies two matrices to give a new one. For sequence problems, we can turn the coefficients into a matrix and use matrix multiplication to simplify the recurrence, and from there find the general term.

## 2. The problem

Suppose we have a sequence with the recurrence $a_{n+1} = pa_n + q^n$ and initial value $a_1$. We want its general term.

### The usual method

We use the method of undetermined coefficients. Given the recurrence

$$
a_{n+1} = pa_n + q^n
$$

our goal is to find a form

$$
a_{n+1} + xq^{n+1} = p\left(a_n + xq^n\right)
$$

so that comparing the two sides pins down $x$, which in turn leads to the general term.

First, substitute the original recurrence into the left-hand side:

$$
a_{n+1} + xq^{n+1} = pa_n + q^n + xq^{n+1}
$$

Next, we want this to match the right-hand side, $p\left(a_n + xq^n\right) = pa_n + pxq^n$. Comparing the terms on both sides gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
q^n + xq^{n+1} &= pxq^n \\
q^n(1 + xq) &= pxq^n \\
1 + xq &= px
\end{aligned}
$$

that is (this requires $p \neq q$)

$$
x = \frac{1}{p - q}
$$

So once we have such an $x$, we can build a new sequence $b_n = a_n + xq^n$, which satisfies the simpler recurrence $b_{n+1} = pb_n$.

Finally, we can find the general term of $b_n$, and then get that of the original sequence from $a_n = b_n - xq^n$. Concretely,

$$
b_n = a_n + \frac{q^n}{p-q}
$$

Since $b_{n+1} = pb_n$, this is a geometric sequence with ratio $p$ and first term $b_1 = a_1 + \dfrac{q}{p-q}$, so

$$
b_n = b_1 \cdot p^{n-1} = \left(a_1 + \frac{q}{p-q}\right) p^{n-1}
$$

This gives the general term of $a_n$:

$$
\boxed{a_n = \left(a_1 + \frac{q}{p-q}\right) p^{n-1} - \frac{q^n}{p-q}}
$$

## 3. Going further

Throughout the solution, what we keep transforming is the coefficients. Is this kind of transformation tied to some mathematical tool?

In fact, linear operations on complex numbers and vectors have similar properties. For example, a complex number can be represented as a two-dimensional vector, and operations such as vector addition and scalar multiplication can all be expressed with matrices.

### Linear operations on complex numbers and vectors

A complex number can be seen as a special case of a two-dimensional vector, with the real part as the vector's x component and the imaginary part as its y component. Adding and multiplying complex numbers resemble linear combinations in a vector space.

Linear operations in a vector space include vector addition, scalar multiplication and linear combinations of vectors. These operations can be represented with matrices, and matrix multiplication provides a way to carry out such transformations.

### An example

Suppose we have two complex numbers $z_1 = a + bi$ and $z_2 = c + di$, where $a, b, c, d$ are all real.

#### Addition

Adding complex numbers can be written as

$$
z_1 + z_2 = (a + bi) + (c + di) = (a + c) + (b + d)i
$$

and we can represent the same operation with matrices:

$$
\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ -b & a \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} c & d \\ -d & c \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} a+c & b+d \\ -(b+d) & a+c \end{pmatrix}
$$

#### Subtraction

Subtracting complex numbers can be written as

$$
z_1 - z_2 = (a + bi) - (c + di) = (a - c) + (b - d)i
$$

and likewise, with matrices:

$$
\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ -b & a \end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix} c & d \\ -d & c \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} a-c & b-d \\ -(b-d) & a-c \end{pmatrix}
$$

## 4. Solving the original problem with matrices

Let's solve the original problem again, this time with matrices. Define the state vector $\mathbf{s}_n$ and build the matrix $A$:

$$
\mathbf{s}_n = \begin{pmatrix} a_n \\ q^n \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A = \begin{pmatrix} p & 1 \\ 0 & q \end{pmatrix}
$$

so that $\mathbf{s}_{n+1} = A\,\mathbf{s}_n$, and hence $\mathbf{s}_n = A^{n-1}\mathbf{s}_1$. The problem becomes computing the matrix power $A^{n-1}$.

Since $A$ is upper triangular, its powers are upper triangular too, with powers of $p$ and $q$ on the diagonal:

$$
A^{n-1} = \begin{pmatrix} p^{n-1} & * \\ 0 & q^{n-1} \end{pmatrix}
$$

To find the top-right entry, work out the first few powers:

$$
A^2 = \begin{pmatrix} p^2 & p + q \\ 0 & q^2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A^3 = \begin{pmatrix} p^3 & p^2 + pq + q^2 \\ 0 & q^3 \end{pmatrix}
$$

In general (this can be proved by induction),

$$
A^{n-1} = \begin{pmatrix} p^{n-1} & \sum_{k=0}^{n-2} p^{n-2-k}q^k \\ 0 & q^{n-1} \end{pmatrix}
$$

The initial value is $a_1$, so the initial state vector is $\mathbf{s}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} a_1 \\ q \end{pmatrix}$. Substituting $A^{n-1}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{s}_n &= \begin{pmatrix} p^{n-1} & \sum_{k=0}^{n-2} p^{n-2-k}q^k \\ 0 & q^{n-1} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} a_1 \\ q \end{pmatrix} \\
&= \begin{pmatrix} a_1 p^{n-1} + q \sum_{k=0}^{n-2} p^{n-2-k}q^k \\ q^n \end{pmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Taking the first component:

$$
a_n = a_1 p^{n-1} + q \sum_{k=0}^{n-2} p^{n-2-k}q^k
$$

Notice that the sum in it is the sum of a geometric series:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{k=0}^{n-2} p^{n-2-k}q^k &= p^{n-2} \sum_{k=0}^{n-2} \left(\frac{q}{p}\right)^k \\
&= p^{n-2} \cdot \frac{1 - \left(\frac{q}{p}\right)^{n-1}}{1 - \frac{q}{p}} \\
&= \frac{p^{n-1}}{p-q} \left(1 - \left(\frac{q}{p}\right)^{n-1}\right)
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
a_n &= a_1 p^{n-1} + \frac{q\,p^{n-1}}{p-q} \left(1 - \left(\frac{q}{p}\right)^{n-1}\right) \\
&= a_1 p^{n-1} + \frac{q\,p^{n-1}}{p-q} - \frac{q^n}{p-q}
\end{aligned}
$$

Factoring out the common term gives the same result as the method of undetermined coefficients:

$$
\boxed{a_n = \left(a_1 + \frac{q}{p-q}\right) p^{n-1} - \frac{q^n}{p-q}}
$$

So the matrix method offers a new way of looking at recurrences.
